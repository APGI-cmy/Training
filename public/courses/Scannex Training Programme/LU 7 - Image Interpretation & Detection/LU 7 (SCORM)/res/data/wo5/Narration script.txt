INTRO
Normal Anatomy and Lookalikes. Learning outcome: Recognise normal anatomical patterns and explain why they can resemble concealed objects. Work through the nine illustrated anatomy topics. Use position, symmetry, repetition, texture and surrounding structures to explain a dense feature before deciding that it is suspicious. Each image can be enlarged.

SUMMARY
Key takeaway: Normal vertebrae and spinal repetition have organised features. The C1 arch, bone texture, body position and overlapping joints can alter appearance. Carpal and tarsal bones and the patella must be recognised in their expected locations. Naturally occurring gallstones need an anatomical explanation, while the ear canal is a location that warrants deliberate review. Combine several clues and seek authorised help when an area remains unexplained.

STATE-01
Vertebral anatomy. The vertebral body, paired pedicles and central spinous process can appear as dense oval or teardrop-shaped features on a frontal scan. Viewed without their wider context, these normal structures can resemble a stone.

Key takeaway: Pedicles occur as a symmetrical pair and the spinous process lies centrally within the vertebral structure. Interpret the whole vertebra, not one dense shape. Vertebral anatomy: paired pedicles and a central spinous process form a normal pattern.

STATE-02
Spinal pattern. Pedicles and spinous processes form repeating, aligned features down the spinal column. This repetition can be mistaken for separate dense objects if the Viewer does not follow the pattern across adjacent vertebrae.

Key takeaway: A normal spinal pattern repeats consistently and symmetrically from the cervical to the lumbar spine. A diamond is more likely to be isolated than repeated. Spinal repetition: alignment and symmetry indicate normal anatomy.

STATE-03
First cervical vertebra. The posterior arch of the first cervical vertebra, also called C1 or the atlas, can resemble a diamond because of the head and neck position during scanning.

Key takeaway: Confirm the expected upper-neck location and its connection to the surrounding cervical anatomy before interpreting a dense feature as suspicious. C1 atlas: position and anatomical connection identify the normal structure.

STATE-04
Bone texture. Bone is not normally uniform inside. Its spongy, trabecular inner structure creates a mottled texture beneath the denser compact outer edge.

Key takeaway: A uniformly dense isolated feature differs from normal mottled bone texture. Compare texture with nearby bone and examine the area in more than one viewing mode. Texture helps distinguish normal bone from a uniformly dense isolated feature.

STATE-05
Position and overlap. The same body part can appear different when viewed from a different angle. Superimposed anatomy can make an area appear denser even when no concealed object is present.

Key takeaway: Check body position and projection. Open hands and correctly positioned feet reduce overlap and help the Viewer assess the wrist, ankle and foot structures accurately. Projection matters: overlapping structures can create apparent density.

STATE-06
Wrist and ankle. Carpal bones at the wrist and tarsal bones at the ankle are compact groups of small, rounded bones. Their outlines or overlap can resemble small dense oval features.

Key takeaway: Confirm the expected wrist or ankle location, grouped pattern and continuity with the hand, forearm, foot and lower-leg anatomy. Carpal and tarsal bones: grouped location and continuity identify normal anatomy.

STATE-07
Patella. The patella, or kneecap, is a pear-shaped bone at the front of the knee. At certain angles it may resemble a comparatively large diamond.

Key takeaway: Check its expected position at the knee and relationship to the normal joint rather than judging its density or shape in isolation. Patella: its position at the front of the knee identifies it.

STATE-08
Gallstones. Gallstones can appear as isolated, stone-like objects and are a recognised anatomical trap. The gallbladder is not part of the route followed by swallowed contents.

Key takeaway: Consider the expected gallbladder location, the surrounding anatomy and any available previous image before treating a stone-like feature as concealed contraband. Gallbladder region: a known location-based anatomical trap.

STATE-09
Ear canal. The external auditory meatus, or ear canal, is an air-filled passage near the outer ear. On a non-inverted scan it normally appears as a small dark oval region.

Key takeaway: Confirm the expected position on each side of the head and the relationship to the surrounding skull anatomy. An unexpected dense appearance should be assessed with the approved viewing and escalation procedure. Ear canal: a dark, symmetrical oval in the expected position is normal anatomy.

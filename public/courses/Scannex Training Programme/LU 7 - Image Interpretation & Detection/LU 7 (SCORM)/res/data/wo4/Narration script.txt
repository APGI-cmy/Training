INTRO
The Digestive Route. Learning outcome: Trace the digestive route and explain why location and movement matter during image interpretation. Follow the route from the mouth to the rectum and anus. The illustrations show each stage separately. Then review peristalsis and the gallbladder caution, so that a dense feature is considered in its anatomical context.

SUMMARY
Key takeaway: The digestive route passes through the mouth, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, and rectum and anus. Peristalsis can change the position of swallowed material over time; movement is one clue, not a conclusion by itself. The gallbladder is outside the direct digestive passage and can contain naturally occurring gallstones. Explain normal anatomy, compare available images and escalate unresolved doubt through the approved process.

STATE-01
Mouth. A swallowed diamond enters through the mouth. Diamonds may also be concealed behind the lips, underneath the tongue or inside the cheeks without being swallowed.

Key takeaway: Interpretation point. The mouth is both the entry point to the digestive tract and a direct concealment area. Consider the location and shape of any apparent object. Mouth: the entry point to the digestive tract.

STATE-02
Esophagus. After swallowing, the object passes down the esophagus towards the stomach. The Viewer should recognise the esophagus as part of the continuous digestive route.

Key takeaway: Interpretation point. An apparent object in this region should be considered in relation to the expected path from the mouth to the stomach. Esophagus: the route from the mouth to the stomach.

STATE-03
Stomach. The object enters the stomach and may remain there temporarily before moving further through the digestive tract. Its position should be evaluated against the expected location of the stomach.

Key takeaway: Interpretation point. Location alone is not enough. Use the viewing tools and any available comparison image to test an unexplained feature. Stomach: a temporary stage in the digestive route.

STATE-04
Small Intestine. The object moves laterally through the twisting loops of the small intestine. Its position may change considerably as digestive contents move.

Key takeaway: Interpretation point. Peristalsis moves contents through the tract. Movement within the digestive contents is a useful clue; a fixed feature needs another explanation. Small intestine: a long, twisting section where position may change.

STATE-05
Large Intestine. The object travels through the large intestine, or colon. The route passes upward and then downward through the abdomen before reaching the rectum.

Key takeaway: Interpretation point. Compare an apparent object with the expected colon pathway rather than interpreting one dense feature in isolation. Large intestine: the route passes up and then down through the abdomen.

STATE-06
Rectum and anus. The object reaches the rectum and is eventually expelled through the anus. The anus is also a frequently used direct concealment site.

Key takeaway: Interpretation point. Distinguish a swallowed object completing the digestive route from a direct concealment indication, then follow the approved response chain if concern remains. Rectum and anus: the end of the digestive route and a direct concealment site.

STATE-07
Timeline Interpretation Note: Peristalsis. The walls of the digestive tract contract in travelling muscular waves called peristalsis. A swallowed object therefore moves over time. Movement within the digestive contents supports the possibility of a swallowed object. A feature that remains fixed against a bone may require a different explanation. Peristalsis moves contents along the digestive route; this illustration locates the small intestine.

STATE-08
Gallbladder Caution. The gallbladder is not part of the route followed by swallowed contents. Gallstones may nevertheless resemble diamonds. The operator must know the gallbladder’s normal position and avoid automatically treating every stone-like feature as concealed contraband. Gallbladder region: a normal anatomical explanation must be considered.

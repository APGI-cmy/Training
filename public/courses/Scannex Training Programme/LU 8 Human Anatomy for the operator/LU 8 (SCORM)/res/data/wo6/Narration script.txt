INTRO
Learning Unit 8: Summary. Learning outcome: Use anatomical position, movement, pattern and texture to explain normal structures and assess unexplained features. Review these points before the final knowledge check. Revisit any activity where you still have uncertainty. Optional Viewer practice supports familiarity with the controls and does not replace the knowledge checks.

SUMMARY
Key takeaway: Use anatomy to prevent false alarms, focus attention on the right areas and support a proportionate, respectful response. The digestive tract and skeleton are the two systems emphasised in this unit. A feature that remains unexplained is an anomaly; assess it through the approved process rather than making a medical diagnosis. The route is mouth, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, then rectum and anus. Peristalsis is travelling muscular contractions that move contents through the tract and can change a swallowed object’s position. Where a reference image is available, compare the same region and account for body position. The gallbladder is outside this route, and gallstones can resemble dense objects. Movement or apparent density alone does not establish what a feature is. Pedicles occur in pairs and spinal structures form a repeating pattern. C1 is the atlas, the first cervical vertebra. Recognise bone texture, overlap caused by body position, carpal bones at the wrist, tarsal bones at the ankle and foot, and the patella at the knee. Consider gallbladder-region densities and the normally dark ear canal. Explain these structures in context and escalate unresolved doubt through the approved process. Check the digestive route and any available evidence of movement. Account for known skeletal anatomy, repeating patterns and symmetry. Confirm expected anatomical position, including the gallbladder region. Compare mottled bone texture with a uniform dense appearance. A feature that remains unexplained is an anomaly. Resolve it through the approved, lawful and dignified response chain.

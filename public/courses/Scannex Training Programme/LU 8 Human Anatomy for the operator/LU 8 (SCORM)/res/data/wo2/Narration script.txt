INTRO
Why Anatomy Matters. Learning outcome: Explain how anatomy helps prevent false alarms, direct the search and support dignity. The operator is not a radiographer and does not diagnose disease. Anatomy is used to account for normal structures so that unexplained anomalies can be assessed calmly and appropriately. An anomaly is a feature that remains unexplained after normal anatomy has been considered. Select each panel to explore the operator’s purpose.

SUMMARY
Key takeaway: Use anatomy to prevent false alarms, focus attention on the right areas and support a proportionate, respectful response. The digestive tract and skeleton are the two systems emphasised in this unit. A feature that remains unexplained is an anomaly; assess it through the approved process rather than making a medical diagnosis.

STATE-01
Prevent false alarms. Many normal bones and organs can appear bright, dense, rounded or oval on a scan. Knowing expected anatomy prevents normal structures from being treated as suspicious. A dense appearance needs an anatomical explanation before it becomes an unexplained anomaly.

STATE-02
Direct the search. Knowing the swallow route and the expected location of normal anatomy helps the operator examine the right areas more carefully. Expected anatomy helps direct the review; appearance alone does not identify the object.

STATE-03
Support dignity. Accurate anatomical recognition reduces unnecessary escalation and supports a lawful, proportionate and respectful response. Anatomical recognition supports dignity and proportionality.

STATE-04
The two systems that matter most. Digestive tract: Relevant because swallowed contents move through a predictable route.

Skeleton: Relevant because several normal bony structures may resemble dense stone-like features. The digestive tract explains a route; the skeleton explains many normal dense appearances.
